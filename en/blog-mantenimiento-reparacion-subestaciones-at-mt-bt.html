Maintenance and repair of HV, MV and LV electrical substations in Mexico

Technical guide to plan inspections, tests and repairs that maintain the electrical reliability of your plant — from low voltage to 69 kV and above.

Read article Request diagnostic
April 27, 2026 · Reading 9 min · By VMS Energy technical team

Why substation maintenance is the most profitable asset of your plant

In the Mexican industrial operation — cement, mining, food and beverage, oil & gas, heavy manufacturing — the substation is the bottleneck between the network and everything else. If it fails, it is not a piece of equipment that fails: the plant fails. Well-planned maintenance is not a recurring expense, it is the cheapest way to buy operational continuity.

This guide explains how to plan, execute and regulate the maintenance of low, medium and high voltage substations in Mexico: types of maintenance, required electrical tests, applicable regulations, typical frequencies and a real case of intervention at 69 kV in a cement plant. At the end you will find a downloadable annual checklist that you can use as the basis of your own plan.

Each hour of unscheduled downtime in a Mexican industrial plant costs between 8 and 120 times what it would cost to have prevented the failure with predictive maintenance. The financial argument for maintenance is no longer discussed — what is discussed is how to do it well.

1. What does the maintenance of an HV, MV and LV substation include?

In Mexican practice, three levels of voltage are handled: Low voltage (bet) up to 1 kV, medium voltage (MV) between 1 and 35 kV (where 13.8 and 23 kV fall, the most common at industrial level) and High voltage (HV) up to 35 kV (69, 115 and 138 kV in connection with CFE). Each level imposes different requirements for insulation, distances, protections and safety procedures.

Maintenance covers all active and passive components of the substation. It's not just "clean and retighten":

  • Power transformers: oil, devanados, bushings, bypass changer, cooling systems.
  • Power switches: operating mechanism, cameras (SF6, vacuum or oil), main contacts and arching.
  • Disconnectors and blades: contacts, mechanisms, alignment.
  • Instrument transformers (TC and TP): isolation, relationship and polarity.
  • Protection systems and reliefs: verification of adjustments, primary and secondary functional tests.
  • Control systems and SCADA: signal integrity, communications, timing.
  • AD/AC/DC auxiliary services: battery bench, chargers, auxiliary distribution.
  • Ground mesh and lightning arrester: resistivity, continuity, integrity of equipment connections.
  • Civil works and structures: supports, foundations, drainage, access control.

If you want to see how a layer substation — power, protection, control, and communications — is designed before planning its maintenance, check our guide for modernisation of substations that goes into detail to model NSIC.

2. Types of maintenance: predictive, preventive and corrective

The three approaches are not alternatives — they are complementary and must coexist in a mature plan. The difference is how much OPEX they absorb and how much residual risk they leave behind.

Focus When it applies Data/tools Typical frequency Residual risk
Predictive Normal operation, without shutdown. Thermography, online monitoring, oil analysis (DGA), partial downloads. Continuous or quarterly. Low — anticipates failures before they escalate.
Preventive Planned scheduled stoppage. Electrical tests, visual inspection, retightening, lubrication, calibration. Annual to biannual. Medium — depends on quality of the routine.
Corrective After a malfunction in operation. Root cause diagnosis, spare parts, repair or replacement. Ad-hoc. High — more expensive, slower, riskier.

The typical mistake is to execute only preventive measures "because it has always been done that way" without implementing predictive measures. A $2,000 bi-annual thermography can detect a hot spot that prevents an $800,000 transformer fire plus three weeks of downtime.

3. Key electrical tests

These are the tests that any serious industrial substation maintenance plan must consider. The first three are non-invasive (predictive); the rest require de-energized equipment.

Infrared Thermography

Inspection with thermal camera of bars, connections, bushings and mechanisms in operation. Detects hot spots due to poor tightening, corrosion or overload before they escalate to failure. It is the test with the best cost-benefit ratio in the catalogue.

Insulation resistance (megger) and dielectric absorption

Measures the integrity of solid insulation in transformers, cables and motors by applying DC voltage. The polarization index (PI) and dielectric absorption ratio (DAR) indicate moisture or contamination of the insulation.

Power factor and tan-delta

In power transformers, bushings and cables. Detects insulation aging due to humidity, contamination or thermal degradation. An upward trend in successive measurements is an early sign of loss of useful life.

Physical-chemical and dielectric analysis of the oil

Transformer oil is a silent witness of its internal health. Dissolved gas analysis (DGA) by chromatography can detect arcing, partial discharge or overheating of paper and oil. Tests of dielectric strength (BDV), humidity, acidity and interfacial voltage complete the diagnosis.

Switch contact resistance (DLRO)

Measures voltage drop with high test current (100−600 A DC) in main contacts of switches and disconnectors. Abnormal resistance indicates wear, oxidation, or lack of contact pressure — precursors to heating and arcing.

Mesh grounding and continuity measurements

It checks the effectiveness of the substation's land system. A degraded mesh is an invisible safety risk: crossing and contact voltage can become lethal without notice. Soil resistance, mesh resistance and continuity of equipment connections are measured.

Test What it detects Reference standard Recommended frequency
Thermography Hot spots, poor tightening, overload. NETA MTS, ASTM E1934 Semiannual
Insulation resistance Humidity, pollution, degradation. IEEE 43, IEC 60270 Annual
Power factor / tan-delta Aging of insulation. IEEE C57.152 Annual to biannual
Oil analysis (DGA + FQ) Arcing, discharge, overheating. NMX-J-169-ANCE, IEEE C57.106, IEC 60422 Annual (semi-annual in critics)
Contact resistance (DLRO) Wear, oxidation. IEC 62271 Annual to biannual
Land system Step and touch voltages; continuity. IEEE 80, IEEE 81, NOM-001-SEDE Biennial

4. Applicable regulations in Mexico

The regulatory framework for industrial electrical maintenance in Mexico combines mandatory Mexican NOMs and international standards adopted by practice:

  • NOM-001-SEDE-2012 — electrical installations for use. General framework mandatory for any industrial installation in Mexico.
  • NOM-029-STPS-2011 — Maintenance of electrical installations in workplaces — Obligations of the employer for safe operation and maintenance.
  • NMX-J-169-ANCE — Test methods for distribution and power transformers.
  • IEEE 62/IEEE C57.152 — Diagnostic guides for liquid-isolated transformers, widely adopted at industrial level.
  • IEC 60076 — Specifications for power transformers.
  • IEC 60270 — Techniques for measuring partial discharges in isolation.
  • CFE-G0000-62 and NRF standards of PEMEX when applying to the customer.
  • NFPA 70E — Electrical safety in workplaces, analysis of the categories 1-4 of the Arthur flash and EPP.

For the industrial client, the important thing is not to memorize the list — it is to require that the maintenance provider provide documentary evidence of compliance for each one. A serious report includes signed protocols, calibration certificates of the test equipment and a technical opinion against the standard.

5. Recommended frequencies and annual plan

The annual plan must combine frequent short inspections with large spaced interventions. A reference matrix for 13.8−69 kV industrial substations in Mexico:

Team Visual inspection Electrical tests Major maintenance
Power transformer Monthly Semi-annual DGA, annual dielectrics Every 6 years
Power switch (SF6) Quarterly Annual DLRO and operating time Every 5 years or according to the number of operations
Disconnectors and blades Quarterly Bi-annual DLRO Every 6-8 years
Protection relays N/A Annual functional tests Recalibration every 4-5 years
DC battery bank Monthly Annual Conductance and Discharge Replacement every 8-12 years
Ground mesh Annual visual Biennial resistivity and resistance Refreshment every 10 years

Numbers are typical industrial reference. The criticality of the process, environmental conditions (corrosives, altitude, temperature) and the history of the substation can adjust frequencies in both directions.

6. Signs that your substation needs urgent repair

If your substation presents any of these signals, do not wait for the next scheduled stoppage:

  • Repeated or untimely shootings without identifiable cause.
  • Abnormal noises in the transformer (louder than usual hum, internal metallic knocks).
  • Smell of burning oil or insulation.
  • Visible oil leaks in transformers, bushings or tanks.
  • Abnormal thermal readings in online monitoring or thermography.
  • Buchholz, overpressure or low oil level alarms active.
  • Visible heating or discoloration on bars and connections.
  • Difficulty operating switches or disconnectors (sticking, knocking, out-of-spec operating times).
  • Presence of audible or visible partial discharges (corona effect).
  • Unexplained variations in voltage, current or power factor readings.

Any of these require immediate corrective intervention with qualified personnel, not waiting for the next schedule. The cost of acting late escalates exponentially.

7. Real case — major maintenance 69 kV cement plant Jalisco (2025)

Operation case · 2025

Major maintenance to 69 kV substation — cement plant, Jalisco

Context: Jalisco’s cement plant with 69/13.8 kV substation feeding rotary furnaces, mills and auxiliary services. Operational criticism requires that any intervention be executed within a planned stop window, which is produced months in advance.

Scope executed by VMS Energy:

  • Comprehensive dielectric tests on the 69/13.8 kV power transformer: power factor, insulation resistance, physical-chemical analysis and DGA of the oil.
  • Major maintenance on power switches: review of mechanisms, contacts, timing test and DLRO resistance.
  • Certified retightening of power connections with calibrated torque and photographic record.
  • Pre-intervention and post-intervention termography to validate result.
  • Verification and refresh of protections, including functional tests of relays and coordination check.
  • Inspection and measurements of the ground mesh, with opinion against IEEE 80.
  • Complete documentation: signed protocols, technical opinions and log delivery.

Result: substation returned to operation within the planned window, with favorable acceptance tests and a predictive 12-month follow-up plan. The client maintained the continuity of the production line and obtained a technical dossier for ISO and corporate reporting.

8. Maintenance or modernization? How to decide

There comes a point when maintenance no longer restores useful life — it only postpones the inevitable. These are the most used discernment criteria:

  • Technical age: equipment with more than 35 years of service in MV or more than 40 in HV enters into structural assessment area.
  • Trend of dielectric tests: If the power factor and DGA show accelerated degradation in successive measurements, the insulation is no longer recovered.
  • Availability of spare parts: Discontinued equipment with global stock < 6 months are increasing operational risk.
  • Capacity and growth: If the plant plans expansion, modernise to 1.5x the current .. . . avoids a second intervention in 3 years.
  • Regulatory gaps: obsolete electromechanical protections, communications without SCADA, uncertifiable meshes — the cost of remedying can come close to replacing.

If after diagnosis the problem is structural — design, capacity, or obsolete protections — applies our self-diagnosis of modernization which orders the layered decision (NSIC) and translates technical findings to CAPEX and OPEX actuable.

9. How to choose an electrical maintenance provider

Five practical criteria to evaluate a supplier before signing an annual contract:

  1. Current ISO certifications: 9001 (quality), 14001 (environmental), 45001 (OSH), 37001 (anti-bribery) and 50001 (energy) are floor, not plus.
  2. Calibrated and traceable test equipment: requires existing calibration certificates against national standards (EC133 or equivalent).
  3. Multi-brand capability: ABB, Siemens, Schneider, GE, Hitachi and similar equipment can be involved without involving a single manufacturer.
  4. Documented experience in your sector: verifiable references in the cement companies, mining companies, food or the sector that applies.
  5. Comprehensive EPC Capability: If the diagnosis reveals that you need modernization, you should have a supplier who can execute engineering, supply, and construction — not to re-bring between three different contractors.

11. Frequently asked questions

How often should an industrial substation be maintained?

Monthly or quarterly visual inspection, major electrical tests every 12 to 24 months and major maintenance with scheduled shutdown every 4 to 6 years is a reference scheme. The exact plan depends on process criticality, stress levels, and predictive analysis results.

What is the difference between preventive, predictive and evolutive maintenance?

Predictive is based on data and continuous monitoring to anticipate failures. The preventive follows a schedule of periodic inspections and tests. The corrective intervenes when the failure has already occurred. The logical order to minimize OPEX is predictive, then preventive and only corrective in emergencies.

What electrical tests are mandatory for substations in Mexico?

NOM-001-SEDE-2012 and NOM-029-STPS-2011 establish the general framework. In industrial practice, thermography, insulation resistance, power factor, dielectric oil analysis (NMX-J-169-ANCE) and contact resistance in switches are mandatory.

Can maintenance be done without stopping the operation?

Predictive inspections such as thermography, partial discharge analysis and online monitoring are executed with the substation energized. Major electrical tests and intervention on transformers and switches require programmed shutdown and safe lockout (LOTO).

How long does a stoppage require a longer maintenance?

A maintenance greater than a substation of 13.8 to 69 kV usually requires between 24 and 72 hours of scheduled stoppage, depending on the range, number of bays and if intervention is included to the power transformer. Planning with the client is critical to minimize the window.

What regulations do the dielectric oil of transformers cover?

NMX-J-169-ANCE defines test methods for mineral insulating oil. IEEE C57.106 and IEC 60422 are the most used international references for dissolved gas diagnostic and acceptance criteria (DGA).

Does annual maintenance or event care contract agree?

For critical processes, an annual contract with a predictive and preventive plan is more economical than event-based care. Intervention by event is only appropriate in secondary facilities with low criticality or with a nearby modernization plan that will make the current equipment obsolete.

Maintenance or replacement of the transformer?

If dielectric, oil analytical and thermography tests indicate structural degradation of the insulation, maintenance no longer recovers useful life and modernization should be evaluated. guide to modernization and self-diagnosis help decide between intervention and replacement.
