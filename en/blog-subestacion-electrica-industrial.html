When and why is an electric substation needed?

From operational need to informed technical decision making

September 5, 2025·Industrial power stations

“Discover the critical detonators for installing an industrial power substation and how to optimize CAPEX and OPEX through a layer-based design.”

Conceptual Diagram:

Introduction

In environments energy and infrastructure, the growth of an operation is rarely linear. Changes in electrical demand, the incorporation of increasingly critical processes or obsolescence of existing infrastructure often lead to a key question for areas of Engineering, Operations and Procurement:

When does a substation become necessary?

Answering this question does not only involve selecting equipment or defining a scope of work. It involves understanding what is a substation for, how it is integrated into existing industrial electrical system and what technical decisions will condition their reliability, operating costs and future performance.

Does your substation face these challenges?

The need for an electrical substation

In practice, a substation becomes necessary when the existing electrical system Stop responding reliably to the actual conditions of the operation.

These scenarios usually occur gradually, but their effects manifest abruptly in operation.

01

Growth or productive expansion

Increases in productive capacity, new process lines or expansions that exceed the installed electric capacity, generating operating limits or overload risks.

02

Recurrent failures and operational discontinuity

Recurrent faults, untimely trips or electrical events that affect the Operational continuity and the stability of the production process.

03

Technical obsolescence of assets

Equipment with expired useful life, outdated technologies or systems that no longer comply with Current operating and safety requirements.

04

Critical operation or strategic energy

Operations where electrical energy becomes strategic and where an electrical event has a direct impact on safety, production and compliance.

What to do?

When any of these triggers appear, the question stops being whether an electrical substation is needed, and becomes how a system that responds to the present and the future should be designed.

Technical analysis model

Think of the substation as a layered system

Beyond a set of equipment, an electrical substation must be analyzed as a integrated system, where each technical layer fulfills a specific function and determines the overall performance.

  • Analyze the system in an orderly manner, without losing the comprehensive vision
  • Understand how technical decisions impact the operation and the business
  • Anticipate risks, limitations and trade-offs before execution

Technical elements (engineering, integration, scoping decisions)

    The technical layers and their impact on the operation

    Each layer fulfills a specific function, but their true value appears when they are correctly integrated into the electrical system. In this section we will review the operational impact from a perspective CAPEX / OPEX, understanding that technical decisions have direct implications on costs, risks and performance.

    • CAPEX: groups investment decisions that define the architecture, capacity and configuration of the system during the design and execution phases of the project.
    • OPEX: materializes throughout the operational life of the system and is directly influenced by CAPEX decisions, impacting maintenance, efficiency, reliability and availability.

    Since Project Management, the optimal decision is not based on CAPEX u OPEX in isolation, but in its joint impact on the Total cost of ownership (TCO) and the performance of the system throughout its life cycle, which is addressed in the following section

    01

    Power Layer

    CAPEX OPEX Risks

    Impact on capacity and architecture of the system (CAPEX) and in its maintenance, efficiency and operational losses (OPEX).

    Typical technical impact on CAPEX
    • Sizing of transformers, switchgear and bars
    • Civil works / space / future expansion
    • Technology selection (AIS/GIS), voltage levels
    Typical technical impact on OPEX
    • Losses, heating and operational efficiency
    • Accessibility, maneuverability and maintenance times
    • Spare parts, MTBF and shutdown windows

    Since Project Management, these failures are not isolated elements, but the result of design and integration decisions made in early stages

    Typical faults
    • Oversize that can increase unnecessary costs
    • Sub-dimensioning which limits the operation from day one.
    • In design and installation of electrical insulation
    • Deficiencies or discrepancies in connections
    • In calculation and installation of grounding systems
    • Errors in selection and integration of power equipment
    02

    Protection Layer

    CAPEX OPEX Risks

    Impact on protection scheme and scope CAPEX and selectivity, diagnostic capacity and operational recovery timesOPEX.

    Typical technical impact on CAPEX
    • Instrument transformers (TC/TP) and their precision class
    • Protection architecture (schemes, redundancy, backup)
    • Trip circuits, tests and associated wiring
    Typical technical impact on OPEX
    • Diagnostic time and location of faults
    • False shots / unavailability due to poor coordination
    • Quality of event logs and periodic adjustment

    Since Project Management, these deviations usually originate in design and coordination decisions taken at early stages of the project. In this layer is defined how faults are detected and isolated, poorly coordinated protection does not usually fail in tests, but rather during actual operation.

    Typical faults
    • Lack of selectivity between protections
    • Incorrect or inconsistent protection settings
    • Incorrect choice in trip circuits
    • Unverified ancillary services
    03

    Control Layer

    CAPEX OPEX Integration

    Impact on control architecture and ancillary services (CAPEX);and in the operability, safety and consistency of the operation OPEX.

    Typical technical impact on CAPEX
    • Bunch, logic, interlocking and sequences
    • AC/DC auxiliary services (back up and continuity)
    • HMI / local controls / signaling and alarms
    Typical technical impact on OPEX
    • Human errors due to ambiguous or incomplete operation
    • Replenishment time in case of contingency (procedures and logic)
    • Availability by quality of auxiliaries and maintenance

    Since Project Management These deviations rarely manifest as electrical failures, but as operational problems directly affecting the reliability of the system.

    Typical faults
    • Incorrect sequences of operation
    • Incomplete or inconsistent interlocks
    • Incorrect information
    • Overdependence on human personnel
    04

    Communications Layer

    CAPEX OPEX Risks

    Impact on network infrastructure and supervision (CAPEX) and on the visibility, traceability and operational response capacity data-based (OPEX).

    CAPEX typically impacts
    • Network topology, channeling and communication points
    • Monitoring/Shamp (monitoring, alarms, trends)
    • Time synchronization and event quality
    OPEX typically impacts
    • Remote diagnosis vs. site visits (time and continuity)
    • Incident traceability and post-event analysis
    • Risk of “operating blind” due to lack of reliable data

    Since Project Management a poor communications architecture is not usually stop the system, but it does limit the ability to understand it, diagnose it and make timely decisions.

    Typical Failures
    • Loss of communication
    • Lack of synchronization
    • Incomplete integration into supervisory systems
    • Operation without information

    The Fifth Layer

    Full integration as an approach to reduce technical and operational risks.

    The Fifth Layer is not an additional technology. It is a whole system integration and accountability approach.

    Turnkey / Key in Hand Project

    What is it?

    Since Project Management, the Fifth Layer coordinates decisions of Power, Protection, Control and Communications for the system to operate as a coherent unit, and not as the sum of independent solutions.

    It is commonly materialized under schemes Turnkey / Key in Hand Project, where responsibility for system performance lies with a single integrator.

    Get off me:
    • “Ownerless” interfaces between providers, disciplines and stages.
    • Re-work and late changes that become OPEX during commissioning and operation.
    • Isolated decisions that compromise continuity, security and scalability.
    Key idea: When no one owns the entire system, the risk is fragmented and materialized in operation.

    Why does the Fifth Layer arise?

    • Many operational risks do not originate from equipment failures, but in unresolved interfaces.
    • Much of the OPEX and the operational risk is defined before the purchase of the first equipment.
    • The most costly deviations appear when decisions are made by layer, supplier or discipline, without a systemic vision.
    The Fifth Layer arises in response to this pattern: when integration is not explicitly managed, problems appear at start-up and become OPEX.

    What does the Fifth Layer integrate?

    • Electrical architecture (capacity, redundancy, growth).
    • Protection schemes and coordination.
    • Control logic, auxiliary services and human operation.
    • Communications, supervision, synchronization and traceability.
    • Interfaces between disciplines, suppliers and project stages.
    Ensures transversal integration between technical layers, aligning engineering, procurement and work decisions under the same performance criteria.

    Impact of the Fifth Layer on CAPEX, OPEX and Risks

    CAPEX
    • Reduces rework and late changes.
    • Avoid defensive oversizing.
    • Clarifies scopes, interfaces and responsibilities.
    OPEX
    • Stabilizes operation from startup.
    • Reduces diagnosis and recovery times.
    • Reduces human errors and operational ambiguities.
    Risks
    • Reduces the probability of unscheduled stoppages.
    • Reduces risks to equipment and people.
    • Reduces high impact electrical events.
    From a perspective of Project Management, this changes the focus: from managing contracts or isolated disciplines, to managing the comprehensive system performance throughout its life cycle, enabling evaluation by risk assumed, Expected performance and Total cost of ownership (TCO).

    When does it make sense to apply the Fifth Layer?

    • The project involves multiple suppliers or technologies.
    • Costs due to downtime or unavailability are high.
    • Registration required reliability, safety or traceability.
    • The strategic plan includes growth or adaptations future.
    • The aim is to reduce risks from the design, do not correct them in operation.
    When system integration is managed explicitly, the project stops depending on late fixes and begins to generate value from its start-up.

    Frequently asked questions about industrial electrical substations

    Brief answers to support informed technical decision making.

    What is the use of an electric substation in an industrial facility?

    An electric substation allows receive, transform, protect and distribute The purpose of the power supply is to adapt the energy to the actual conditions of the operation, guaranteeing continuity, safety and the capacity to grow the industrial electrical system.

    When does an electric substation become necessary?

    A substation becomes necessary when the existing electrical system Stop responding reliably This usually happens when the load growth, plant expansion, critical processes are incorporated, recurring reliability problems or when the installed infrastructure reaches the end of its service life.

    What is the difference between designing a substation by equipment and designing it by layers?

    Designing a substation in layers involves analyzing the system as an integrated whole—power, protection, control, and communications—rather than evaluating equipment in isolation. This approach allows anticipate impacts on CAPEX, OPEX and operational risks, facilitating more informed and sustainable technical decisions throughout the project life cycle.

    How does a poorly designed substation impact operating costs (OPEX)?

    A poorly integrated substation can generate higher maintenance costs, technical losses, unavailability times, reprocessing and difficulties for future expansions. Many decisions that appear to reduce the initial investment end up increasing OPEX over the entire life of the system.

    What risks exist if the protective and control layers are not properly evaluated?

    The lack of adequate evaluation in these layers can lead to Unscheduled shutdowns, untimely trips, difficulty diagnosing faults, risks to people and slow system recovery from electrical events. These risks usually manifest themselves during operation, not in the construction stage.

    In what cases should an EPC project or turnkey for a substation be evaluated?

    A turnkey EPC scheme or project is usually convenient when the substation is critical for the operation, there is high technical complexity or multiple interfaces between disciplines. This approach helps centralize technical responsibility, coordinate testing and commissioning, and reduce risks associated with project fragmentation.

    What is a previous technical diagnosis and why is it important before a substation project?

    A previous technical diagnosis allows order engineering, operation and procurement criteria Its objective is not to impose an execution model, but to identify risks, technical alternatives and real project scopes from an integrated perspective.
