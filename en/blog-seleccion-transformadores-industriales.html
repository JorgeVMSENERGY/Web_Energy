How to select industrial transformers: technical criteria and decision keys

The transformer data sheet is decided in engineering, but selection errors are paid for in operation. This guide covers all the parameters that determine whether a transformer will serve correctly for 25 years or create problems from the first year.

Read article Request specification
May 1, 2026 · Reading 11 min · By VMS Energy technical team

The transformer that no one specified correctly

In most industrial projects, the transformer is treated as a standard component: the load is estimated, the nearest kVA is ordered, and it is ordered. The problem appears six months later, when the equipment operates hot in summer, the protection relays do not coordinate with the real fault current, or the harmonics of the frequency converters deteriorate the insulation prematurely.

Correctly selecting an industrial transformer is not searching through a catalog — it is solving a system of equations where power, voltage level, vector group, impedance, cooling type, altitude and load profile interact. This guide goes through each parameter with criteria applicable to real projects in Mexico.

A mis-specified transformer does not fail to strike: silently degrades its insulation, reduces its service life by 30 to 60%, raises losses from the NOC and can invalidate the coordination of protections of the entire substation.

1. Types of industrial transformers

Before entering into parameters, it is worth distinguishing the two large categories that define the entire selection conversation:

Distribution

Distribution transformers

From 10 kVA to 5 MVA. They are the link between the medium voltage network (13.8, 23 or 34.5 kV) and the low voltage panels (480 V or 220 V) of the plant. They are the most frequent in the manufacturing industry, food, medium mining and oil & gas.

Power

Power transformers

From 5 MVA onwards, generally at high voltage levels (69 kV, 115 kV, 230 kV). They are the interconnection point with the CFE network for large transmission plants or substations. Its specification requires a short circuit study and coordination of protections at the system level.

Within both categories there are two insulation technologies:

Parameter In mineral oil Dry type (epoxi resin or cast resin)
Typical installation External substations, semi-intemperie, industrial open dinghies Fire-restricted interiors: buildings, hospitals, offshore platforms, shopping centers
Common power range 10 kVA – 400 MVA 15 kVA – 20 MVA (higher ranges on request)
Relative cost Less at high powers 15–30% higher than in oil at the same power
Maintenance Requires periodic oil analysis (DGA, stiffness, moisture) Less intervention; visual inspection and cleaning
Permissible overload Higher thermal overload capacity Less tolerance to prolonged overloads
Environmental risk Requires containment dam and oil separators No risk of spillage; more environmentally friendly

2. How to calculate the required power in kVA

The most frequent error is sizing the transformer equal to the sum of the installed powers of the equipment. This ignores that all equipment never operates simultaneously at 100% and that the load is expressed in kW while the transformer is sized in kVA.

Step 1 — Actual demand load

Total installed load (kW) = sum of nominal powers of all equipment

Demand factor (DF) = portion of the installed load that operates simultaneously (typical: 0.65–0.85 in Mexican industrial plants)

Actual demand (kW) = Installed load × FD

Ejemplo: 2,800 kW instalados × 0.75 FD = 2,100 kW de demanda real

Step 2 — Conversion to kVA

Average power factor (PF) = system kW to kVA ratio (typical: 0.85–0.92 with capacitor bank)

kVA required = Demanda real (kW) ÷ FP promedio

Ejemplo: 2,100 kW ÷ 0.88 FP = 2,386 kVA

Step 3 — Reserve factor and future growth

A reserve margin of 15 to 25% To absorb plant's fans and unplanned peaks of demand. In EPC projects where the customer anticipates growth in 5 years, the margin can reach 30%.

design kVA = kVA requeridos × (1 + factor de reserva)

Example: 2,386 kVA × 1.20 = 2,863 kVA → select 3,000 kVA transformer (closest standard rating)

The transformers are manufactured in standardized capacities according to IEC 60076-1: … 1,000 / 1,250 / 1,600 / 2,000 / 2,500 / 3,150 / 4,000 / 5,000 kVA … The standardized capacity immediately higher than the calculated design kVA is always selected.

3. Voltage level: LV, MV and HV

The primary voltage of the transformer is defined by the level of the network to which it is connected. In Mexico, the most common levels in industrial projects are:

Urban distribution

13.8 kV

The most widespread distribution level of CFE in industrial zones. It covers most industrial parks and manufacturing plants. Dyn11 transformers, ratings from 150 kVA to 3,150 kVA are the most common at this level.

Medium voltage

23kV / 34.5kV

Used in medium capacity distribution and in areas where CFE operates 23 or 34.5 kV networks. Common in some northern states and in infrastructure projects with long lines. 34.5 k V frequently appears in large industrial parks and in solar plant interconnections.

High voltage

69kV / 115kV

Subtransmission level. Applies to large industrial consumers (plants of more than 5 MVA) and energy infrastructure projects. It requires power transformers, its own substation and interconnection agreements with CFE. The specification includes OLTC (on load tap changer) in most cases.

The secondary voltage of the transformer defines the LV network of the plant. In Mexico, standard levels are 480 V three-phase (for motors and power loads) and 220 V three-phase / 127 V single-phase For lighting and service sockets. Some special industrial processes require intermediate voltages (4.16 kV or 6.6 kV) for high-powered engines; in these cases an additional distribution transformer is used or connected directly to MV.

4. Vector group: what it is and when it matters

The vector group defines the configuration of the windings (delta or star) and the angular phase difference between primary and secondary. It is not a trivial fact: it affects the compatibility to operate transformers in parallel, the response to harmonics and the behavior of the differential protection system.

Vector group Configuration Offset Typical application in Mexico
Dyn11 Delta primary, star secondary with neutral 30° delay Industrial distribution standard. Provides neutral in LV, controls 3rd order harmonics, compatible with conventional differential protection.
Yyn0 Star primary, star secondary with neutral 0° CFE distribution networks, power transformers, when it is required to neutralize the primary for ground protection.
Dyn5 Delta primary, star secondary with neutral 150° Parallel with Dyn11 transformers in 12-pulse configurations for harmonic reduction in industrial rectifiers.
Zig-zag (Zyn) Secondary zigzag with neutral Variable Systems with high 3rd order amonic content (unbalanced single phase loads or single cycle VFDs). It offers low zero sequence impedance.

The rule of thumb for EPC projects in Mexico: always specify Dyn11 as a first option unless the short circuit study, parallel configuration or the Ironic analysis indicate another group. Changing the vector group after purchase is impossible without rewinding the transformer.

5. Short circuit impedance

The short circuit impedance (Zcc, expressed as a percentage) is the parameter that is most frequently underestimated in industrial distribution projects. It directly defines the maximum short-circuit current that can flow in the secondary of the transformer, which impacts the required interrupting capacity of all switches and the coordination of protections.

Three-phase short-circuit current in the secondary (kA) = kVA of the transformer ÷ (√3 × Secondary voltage in V × Zcc). For a 2,000 kVA, 480 V and Zcc = 5% transformer: Icc = 2,000,000 ÷ (1.732 × 480 × 0.05) = 48.1kA. That value determines the minimum capacity of the switches on the LV board.

Typical Zcc values per application

  • 4.0%: Small distribution transformers (up to 300 kVA). Higher fault current requires faster switches but lower voltage drop under load.
  • 5.0 – 5.75%: Standard range for industrial distribution from 500 kVA to 3,150 kVA. The most specified in EPC projects in Mexico.
  • 6.0 – 7.5%: Large power transformers or when seeking to limit short-circuit current in LV boards with lower interruption capacity.
  • 10 – 15%: High-voltage, large-capacity power transformers. Differential protection becomes mandatory in these ranges.

The value of Zcc must be defined after The short circuit study, not before. Specifying Zcc without that study is equivalent to blinding switches.

6. Cooling type: ONAN, ONAF and beyond

The cooling system determines how it dissipates heat generated by transformer losses (core losses + winding losses). The IEC 60076-2 standard defines the nomenclature with four letters: internal cooling medium – internal circulation – external cooling medium – external circulation.

IEC code Description Advantages Limitations
ONAN Oil/natural convection — Air/natural convection No moving parts, minimal maintenance, very reliable Greater physical size for the same power
ONAF Oil/natural convection — Air/forced ventilation Increases capacity 25–33% over ONAN with the same windings. Fans are activated automatically by temperature. Fans require periodic maintenance
OFAF Oil / pumps — Air / forced ventilation Maximum cooling capacity, ideal for large power transformers Increased complexity, cost and maintenance
AN (dry) Air/natural convection (oil-free) No risk of spillage, indoor installation, minor civil works Lower surge tolerance, higher cost per kVA
AF (dry) Forced air/ventilation (oil-free) Increases capacity over AN, compact for kVA Fan noise, additional maintenance

In Mexican EPC project practice, 80% of distribution transformers in outdoor industrial substations are specified in ONAN. The change to ONAF is justified when space is limited and the substation needs to be compacted, or when the ambient temperature exceeds 40°C during the summer (common in Sonora, Tamaulipas, Tabasco).

7. Site conditions: altitude, temperature and harmonics

Altitude (derating for more than 1,000 meters above sea level)

At higher altitudes, air density decreases and thermal dissipation capacity is reduced. According to IEC 60076-2, for sites above 1,000 meters above sea level, a correction factor of 0.4% load reduction for every additional 100 meters. In other words, a 2,500 kVA transformer installed in Mexico City (2,240 meters above sea level) will operate efficiently only up to:

Meters above the limit of 1,000 meters above sea level = 2,240 − 1,000 = 1,240 m

Factor de derating = 1,240 m ÷ 100 × 0.4% = 4.96% ≈ 5%

Effective capacity = 2,500 kVA × (1 − 0.05) = 2,375 kVA

If the standard transformer (designed for 1000 msnm) does not cover demand after the derating, there are two solutions: select the upper normalized capacity, or order a transformer specially designed for site altitude, which has an additional cost but optimizes the size.

Ambient temperature

The IEC standard design is for maximum ambient temperature of 40°C. In places such as Culiacán, Hermosillo or Ciudad del Carmen, where summer exceeds 42–45°C in critical months, it is required to specify maximum design temperature of 45°C or 50°C, or dimension in ONAF from the beginning.

Harmonics and K-factor

Variable frequency drives (VFD), rectifiers, switching power supplies and UPS generate harmonic currents that circulate through the transformer winding, increasing their internal thermal losses. A standard transformer (K-1) designed for sinusoidal loading can overheat with non-linear loads even if it operates below its rated kVA.

The K-factor (defined by the IEEE C57.110 standard) quantifies the harmonic content to correctly size the transformer:

  • K-1: Pure sinusoidal charging (without power electronics). Standard.
  • K-4: Mixed load with some VFDs or switching supplies (<50% non-linear load).
  • K-9: Predominantly non-linear load (50–75% VFDs or rectifiers).
  • K-13: Almost completely non-linear load (>75% VFDs, 6-pulse rectifiers).

In EPC projects with drive-intensive process (mining, pumping, belting), specifying high K-factor from the transformer data sheet is mandatory, not optional.

8. Regulations applicable in Mexico

The selection and specification of industrial transformers in Mexico is governed by a mixed framework of national and international standards:

  • IEC 60076 (complete series): The main international reference for power transformers. Part 1 (generalities and tolerances), Part 2 (heating), Part 5 (short circuit resistance), Part 11 (dry type transformers). It is the standard required in most EPC project specifications.
  • NMX-J-116-ANCE: Mexican Standard for distribution transformers (up to 500 kVA, 34.5 kV). Applies to equipment marketed in Mexico and referenced in contracts with CFE.
  • NOM-001-SEDE-2012: Electrical installations (use). Although it is not specific to transformers, it governs the safety distances, grounding and overcurrent protection associated with the installation of the transformer.
  • IEEE C57.12.00: General standard for distribution and power transformers. Frequently required in projects for oil & gas companies or multinationals that apply US standards.
  • IEEE C57.110: Recommended practice for establishing the load level of transformers with non-sinusoidal currents. Reference to specify K-factor.
  • NMX-J-169-ANCE: Mineral oil for transformers. Applies to the analysis and acceptance of the dielectric fluid.

9. Criteria for choosing a manufacturer

The choice of manufacturer should not precede the technical specification — it should be derived from it. Once the data sheet is defined (kVA, voltage, vector group, Zcc, cooling type, altitude, K-factor, design standard), it is evaluated which manufacturer best meets the criteria of: technical scope, delivery time, local support, experience in similar applications and total cost.

Manufacturer Main fortress Typical range Best option when…
DEEMSA National manufacturing, local support, short delivery times and spare parts Oil and dry distribution up to 10 MVA The project requires fast delivery, in-person technical support and agile repairs in Mexico
WEG Broad portfolio in distribution and power, solid regional presence in Latin America 10 kVA – 300 MVA Integration with other WEG equipment (motors, drives) is required or there is a budget restriction on intermediate powers
ABB High-performance technology in HV power transformers, premium energy efficiency Distribution and power, 100 kVA – 1,000 MVA High voltage project (69 kV+), customer requires global brand, or specification requires specific technology (special liquid insulation transformers, high precision OLTC)
SIEMENS Integrated solution with own control systems and SCADA, a benchmark for infrastructure projects Distribution and power, 160 kVA – 1,200 MVA The project includes Siemens control room, unified SCADA, or the client has framework contracts with Siemens for its facilities

On EPC projects, VMS Energy evaluates manufacturers against the project data sheet and site conditions — not against a brand preference. The selection is documented with a technical-economic comparison that remains in the project dossier.

10. Practical case: mining plant in Sonora at 1,500 meters above sea level

EPC engineering case

Distribution transformer selection — Mineral processing plant, Sonora

Site conditions: Altitude 1,500 meters above sea level. Maximum summer temperature: 44°C. Mineral powder in suspension. Semi-outdoor substation.

Load profile:

  • Total installed power: 2,200 kW
  • Estimated demand factor: 0.76 (continuous crushing and belting operation)
  • Average power factor corrected with capacitors: 0.88
  • Non-linear load: 68% (6 frequency converters in grinding circuit and process pump)

Selection calculation:

  • Demanda real: 2,200 × 0.76 = 1,672 kW
  • kVA requeridos: 1,672 ÷ 0.88 = 1,900 kVA
  • With 25% reserve: 1,900 × 1.25 = 2,375 kVA → Standardised capacity: 2,500 kVA
  • Derating by altitude (500 m above 1,000 meters above sea level): 2,500 × 0.98 = 2,450 effective kVA ✓

Defined specifications:

  • Power: 2,500 kVA (ONAN) / 3,000 kVA (ONAF)
  • Voltage: 13.8 kV (primary) / 480 V (secondary)
  • Vector group: Dyn11
  • Impedance: 5.75% (defined after short circuit study)
  • Design temperature: 45°C
  • K-factor: K-9 (68% non-linear loading)
  • Design altitude: 1,500 meters above sea level declared in data sheet
  • Standard: IEC 60076 / NMX-J-116-ANCE

Manufacturer's decision: The combination of altitude, high temperature, K-9 and 8-week delivery time guided the selection towards DEEMSA with a design specification for 1,500 meters above sea level confirmed by the manufacturer. This avoided importing equipment with delivery times of 18–22 weeks and guaranteed local technical support during commissioning.

11. Frequently asked questions

Dry or oil transformer for an industrial plant?

Depends on the environment and application. The oil transformer is the standard option for outdoor or semi-outdoor substations: it is more economical at high power and has better overload capacity. The dry transformer is preferable indoors with fire restrictions (buildings, hospitals, offshore platforms) because it eliminates the risk of oil spills and simplifies civil works by not requiring containment dikes.

What exactly does the Dyn11 vector group mean?

D = primary in delta (delta), and = secundario en estrella, N = neutral available on secondary, 11 = angular offset of 30° clockwise (the minute hand at position 11 on the clock with respect to 12 on the primary). It is the most used vector group in Mexico to power 480 V three-phase loads because it provides neutral in LV, helps control third-order harmonics and is compatible with conventional differential protection.

How does altitude affect the capacity of a transformer?

Less dense air at higher altitudes reduces heat dissipation. In accordance with IEC 60076-2, above 1,000 meters above sea level a derating of ~0.4% nominal load reduction is applied for each additional 100 m. A plant at 1,800 meters above sea level (800 m above the limit) applies a derating of 3.2%, so a 2,500 kVA transformer will operate efficiently up to 2,420 kVA. To avoid this derating, you can request a transformer designed specifically for the declared altitude.

When should K-high factor be specified?

When the load includes variable frequency drives (VFD), rectifiers, switching power supplies, UPS or any power electronics that generate harmonic currents. Rule of thumb: K-4 for mixed loads with few VFDs (<50% non-linear load), K-9 for predominantly non-linear loads (50–75%), K-13 for almost completely non-linear loads (>75%). A K-1 transformer subjected to K-9 load can lose 20–30% of annual useful life due to overheating of the windings.

Does the impedance of the transformer affect short circuit protections?

Yes, directly. A low Zcc (4%) generates higher short circuit current in secondary, which requires higher interrupting capacity switches but reduces the drop of voltage under load. A high Zcc (6–7.5) limits the fault current — useful when the boards of LV have limited interrupting capacity — but increases the drop of voltage during the run thats. The value should define after short-circuit study, not arbitrarily.

What makes DEEMSA, WEG, ABB and Siemens different in industrial transformers?

DEEMSA is a national manufacturer (Mexico) with agile local support, short delivery times and high competitiveness in oil distribution up to 10 MVA. WEG (Brazil) offers a broad portfolio and solid regional presence in Latin America. ABB and Siemens are the global reference for high voltage power transformers (69 kV+) and large-scale projects, with higher costs and delivery times. Selection should be based on project data sheet, not brand preference.

Distribution or power transformer for a 5 MW plant in demand?

With a real demand of 5 MW and power factor of 0.88, the plant requires ~5.7 MVA. Adding 20% ​​reserve: ~6.8 MVA. In this range, we already work with power transformers (conventionally above 5 MVA), which have more robust designs, an under-load tap changer (OLTC) option and a longer projected useful life. The threshold is not fixed: the final decision depends on the primary stress level and the EPC project criteria.
