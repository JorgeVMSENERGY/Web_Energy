Maintenance of transformers in operation: predictive diagnosis and useful life extension

How to read the true condition of a transformer using oil, thermography, and electrical testing—before it fails.

A continuous-service transformer is a machine that cannot be easily stopped for inspection. What happens inside your tank — in oil, paper, core, and devanates — is only accessible through non-invasive diagnostic techniques. The difference between a transformer reaching its 30 years of service life and one that fails catastrophically at 12 is, in most cases, the existence — or absence — of a predictive maintenance program.

This article documents the diagnostic techniques that VMS Energy applies in its maintenance contracts, its interpretation criteria and the decision framework to determine when to rehabilitate and when to replace.

1. Maintenance strategies: corrective, preventive and predictive

Corrective

Repair after failure

Intervention only when the equipment fails. Minimum initial cost, maximum total cost: unscheduled shutdown, collateral damage, emergency replacement time.

Preventive

Intervention by calendar

Oil change, cleaning and adjustment at fixed intervals. Tidier than corrective, but can lead to unnecessary interventions or overlook problems between cycles.

Predictive

Act according to actual condition

Continuous monitoring of equipment status using DGA, thermography, Megger and oil. Intervene only when the data indicates it. Longer useful life, lower total cost.

The predictive approach does not eliminate the preventive one: periodic visual inspection, level verification and external cleaning are still necessary. What predictive adds is the ability to see the true state of the insulation and detect incipient faults before they escalate.

Statistical reference: According to reliability studies on industrial transformers (CIGRE WG A2.37), 74% of catastrophic failures in distribution transformers had generated detectable signals in the DGA at least 6 months in advance. The predictive program converts these signals into time to act.

2. Dielectric oil analysis — the four key tests

Dielectric mineral oil fulfills three simultaneous functions: insulating (electrical support), cooling (heat dissipation) and impregnating the paper (mechanical protection). Over time and operation, its properties degrade. Four parameters are the most informative:

2.1 Dielectric strength (BDV) — IEC 60156

Oil Breakdown Voltage measures the minimum voltage at which oil fails as an insulator on a standardized electrode. Humidity and suspended solid particles are the main factors that reduce it. It is measured with dielectric strength equipment (cup test) by applying increasing voltage until breakdown, averaging 5 or 6 measurements on the same sample.

BDV stiffness (kV/mm) Up to 36 kV (MV) 72.5 kV – 145 kV (HV) Action
≥ 50 Excellent Excellent Continue normal operation
40 – 49 Good Acceptable More frequent monitoring
30 – 39 Alert limit Unacceptable MV: schedule regeneration. HV: immediate action
< 30 Unacceptable Emergency Remove from service. Replace or regenerate oil before re-energizing

2.2 Moisture content (ppm H₂O) — IEC 60814

Water in oil is particularly harmful because it does not remain uniformly distributed: it migrates toward the insulating paper (which has a higher affinity for water) and can concentrate at the oil-paper interface until reaching local critical levels. Oil moisture is measured by Karl Fischer titration and is expressed in parts per million (ppm).

Moisture in oil (ppm)EvaluationRecommended action
< 10Dry oil — optimalContinue normal operation
10 – 20Slightly moistWatch trend
20 – 35Wet — requires attentionProgram drying (vacuum filter)
> 35Saturated — high riskImmediate action. Risk of partial discharges

2.3 Acidity (neutralization number, mgKOH/g) — ASTM D974

With aging, mineral oil oxidizes and generates organic acids that chemically attack the insulating paper and internal metals. Acidity is quantified by the neutralization number (NN): the amount of potassium hydroxide (in mg) needed to neutralize the acids in 1 gram of oil.

  • NN < 0.1 mgKOH/g: New oil or in excellent condition.
  • NN 0.1 – 0.2 mgKOH/g: Normal aging. Continue monitoring.
  • NN 0.2 – 0.4 mgKOH/g: Alert. Regeneration program at next planned stop.
  • NN > 0.4 mgKOH/g: Oil exhausted. Regeneration or change necessary before the deterioration process is irreversible in the paper.

2.4 Interfacial voltage (mN/m) — ASTM D971

Interfacial voltage measures the force at the oil-water interface. In new oil it is high (~45 mN/m); oxidation products and aging reduce it. A value less than 22 mN/m is a sign that the oil is highly oxidized, even if the acidity still appears moderate. It is a useful aging indicator when used in conjunction with the NN.

3. DGA in the field: interpretation of trends

Dissolved gas analysis (DGA) is the diagnostic tool with the greatest predictive value. In the article on FAT/SAT tests (see Blog #2) we describe the gases and their origins. In the context of maintenance in operation, the key element is not the absolute level of each gas but the generation rate: how much it grew since the last sample.

Trend rule (IEEE C57.104): A rate of increase greater than 10% per month in any individual gas, or greater than 30% per month in the Total Fuel Gases (TDCG), indicates an active failure in progression. The recommended action is to increase the sampling frequency to monthly and evaluate the withdrawal from service.

Four conditions TDCG (IEEE C57.104)

Acetylene: the exception to the rule of conditions. Any concentration of acetylene (C₂H₂) above 1 ppm triggers a Condition 4 alert regardless of the total TDCG, because it is the only gas that can only be generated by arcing in the oil. There is no “safe” level of acetylene in a transformer with no prior known arc history.

4. Infrared thermography in transformers

Thermographic inspection is the only diagnostic technique that can be performed while the transformer is in full operation, without the need to disconnect it. It uses an infrared camera to map the temperature distribution on the surface of the equipment and identify abnormal hot spots.

Critical inspection areas

Terminal blocks and HV/LV connections

High priority

Hot spots on terminal blocks indicate loose or rusty connections. A temperature difference greater than 10°C with respect to the symmetric phase is a warning signal. Greater than 30°C is immediate action.

Bushings

High priority

Bushings with degraded insulation generate heat due to dielectric losses. A bushing that exhibits temperature significantly higher than its symmetrical ones (without load difference) must be replaced.

Tap Changer (OLTC)

Medium priority

A tap selector with worn contacts generates localized heat. The OLTC must be inspected with load in the usual working position to make the defect visible.

Radiators and fans

Medium priority

Radiators clogged by dirt or corrosion show non-uniform temperature distributions. Fans with damaged blades or degraded motors manifest themselves in asymmetric thermal loading of the cooling group.

Conditions for a valid inspection

  • Minimum load: The transformer must be operating at a minimum of 40% of its rated load for hot spots to be thermographically detectable.
  • Time of day: Avoid direct sunlight on the equipment; Solar heating masks temperature variations. Prefer inspection on cloudy days or during evening/night hours.
  • Emissivity: The painted metal tank has high emissivity (~0.9). Unoxidized copper or aluminum conductors have low emissivity (~0.05); require parameter correction in the chamber or comparison with contact temperature reference.
  • Distance and angle: Follow NFPA 70B protocol: maximum angle of 45° with respect to the inspected surface to minimize reflections.

5. Megger tests and polarization index (PI)

The Megger test measures the insulation resistance of the transformer dielectric system by applying a DC voltage and measuring the resulting leakage current. It is fast, economical and can be done in the field with the equipment de-energized. However, it has an important limitation: the absolute value of resistance (in MΩ) depends strongly on temperature; Two readings taken at different temperatures are not directly comparable.

The polarization index (PI) overcomes this limitation

The PI compares the insulation resistance measured at 10 minutes with that measured at 1 minute. In healthy insulation, the resistance continues to increase over time (the polarization phenomenon absorbs more current at the beginning). In degraded or wet insulation, the leakage current dominates and the ratio approaches 1.0.

PI = R10min /R1min

PI < 1.0 → Rechazo absoluto  |  PI 1.0–2.0 → Cuestionable  |  PI > 2.0 → Aceptable  |  PI > 4.0 → Excelente

After measurement, completely offloading the winding to ground for a time equal to twice the trial time before touching the terminals. Failure to do so can produce a download that will either damage the staff or the instruments.

6. Insulating paper: the limit that oil cannot exceed

The oil can be regenerated, filtered, treated or replaced. The insulating paper that impregnates the transformer windings cannot be regenerated. Once the cellulose in paper is degraded thermally or by hydrolysis, the loss is irreversible. This makes the status of the paper the most critical factor in the decision to rehabilitate vs. replace.

Life cycle of insulating paper — Degree of Polymerization (DP)
DP 800 – 1,200
New paper

Complete mechanical resistance. High tolerance to short circuit stress. Full useful life ahead.

DP 400 – 799
Aged paper

It has lost some of its flexibility. Oil in good condition can slow down the process. Monitor CO and CO₂ in DGA.

DP < 200
Critical role/end of life

Compromised mechanical resistance. High risk of rupture under next short circuit. Evaluate unit replacement.

How to estimate the condition of the paper without opening the transformer

The DP of paper cannot be measured without taking a physical sample, which requires stopping and opening the equipment. However, there are indirect indicators accessible through oil analysis:

  • CO and CO₂ in DGA: Degrading paper releases carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide into the oil. The CO₂/CO ratio > 11 with CO > 350 ppm suggests role under active thermal stress.
  • 2-Furfuraldehyde (2-FAL): It is the most specific marker of paper aging. It is measured by liquid chromatography. Values ​​> 1 ppm in oil correspond approximately to DP < 400; values ​​> 10 ppm suggest DP < 200. This test, although less common, is the most reliable non-invasive correlation with the actual condition of the paper.

7. Rehabilitation vs. replacement: decision criteria

The question “rehabilitation or replacement?” There is no single answer: it depends on the condition of the paper, the cost of each option, and the operational impact of an unplanned outage. The decision framework that VMS Energy applies weighs three factors:

FactorRehabilitation indicatedIndicated replacement
Paper Status (DP) DP > 400 — paper in good condition DP < 200 — paper in critical condition
Cause of degradation Aged oil, moisture, acidity — fluid problem Internal electrical fault, short circuit between turns, arc
Age of the transformer < 70% of projected useful life (e.g. < 21 years for equipment of 30) > 25 years or unknown
Rehabilitation cost < 60% of the price of the equivalent new unit > 70% of the new unit price
Spare availability New equipment delivery time > 16 weeks New equipment available in < 8 weeks
Energy efficiency Losses within acceptable ranges for TCO Significantly higher losses than equivalent new equipment
Oil rehabilitation — what includes: The dielectric regeneration process comprises: (1) draining the degraded oil, (2) treating or replacing the oil, (3) drying the transformer (vacuum hot oil circulation or heating current in windings), (4) filling with new or dehumidified regenerated oil, and (5) testing for stiffness, humidity, and basal DGA. The process can extend shelf life an additional 10–15 years when the paper is in good condition.

9. Case study: 1,000 kVA transformer with severely degraded oil

Diagnostic case — Beverage plant, Chihuahua

Transformer 1,000 kVA, 13.8 kV / 480 V, Dyn11, ONAN — 18 years in operation

Reason for diagnosis: The transformer had never had oil analysis. The operator detected an unusual dark color in the oil visible through the sight glass. VMS Energy was called for a comprehensive evaluation.

Analysis results (on site + laboratory):

  • Thermograph: Phase B terminal block with ΔT = 28°C with respect to phases A and C. North radiator completely cold (clogged by internal corrosion).
  • Dielectric strength: 24 kV/mm — UNACCEPTABLE for MV
  • Moisture in oil: 48 ppm — Saturated
  • Acidity (NN): 0.52 mgKOH/g — Exhausted oil
  • DGA: H₂: 340 ppm, CH₄: 180 ppm, C₂H₆: 95 ppm, CO: 820 ppm, CO₂: 6,400 ppm — TDCG: 1,835 ppm (Condition 2–3). Without acetylene.
  • 2-Furfuraldehyde (2-FAL): 1.8 ppm — suggests estimated DP ~350 (aged but still functional paper)
  • Polarization index: PI = 2.4 — Acceptable with caution

Diagnosis: The oil is severely degraded (high humidity, high acidity, low stiffness) and has begun to affect the paper (elevated CO and 2-FAL). However, the paper is in Still Recoverable condition (estimated DP ~350, no acetylene, PI acceptable). The phase B terminal block requires retightening or immediate replacement; The north radiator requires cleaning or replacement.

Decision: Rehabilitation indicated. The cost of new oil, vacuum drying, terminal block repair and radiator cleaning represented 22% of the price of equivalent new equipment. After treatment, the BDV rose to 58 kV/mm and the humidity dropped to 7 ppm. Follow-up analysis was scheduled at 3 and 6 months with monthly DGA monitoring during the first semester.

Frequently asked questions about transformer maintenance

How often should a transformer's oil be analyzed?

As a general rule according to IEC 60422: new transformers in the first two years, once a year. Equipment in continuous service without anomalies: every 12–24 months. Equipment with a history of problems, high load or aggressive environment: every 6 months. When the DGA shows an upward trend in any key gas: monthly frequency until stabilizing. VMS Energy recommends the first analysis within the first 90 days of operation as a baseline.

What difference is there between the Megger test and the polarization index?

The Megger test measures insulation resistance (MΩ/GΩ) for 60 seconds. It is fast but sensitive to temperature. The polarization index (PI) is the ratio R10min/R1min, which eliminates the temperature effect and reveals the real state of the insulation: PI < 1.0 indicates degradation or severe humidity; PI > 2.0 is acceptable; PI > 4.0 is excellent.

When should a transformer be rehabilitated rather than replaced?

Rehabilitation is economical when the cost does not exceed 60–70% of the new equipment and when the insulating paper is in good condition (DP > 400). If the paper has DP < 200, no amount of oil rehabilitation will solve the problem. Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) analysis is the correct decision framework.

How reliable is the short-term to detect problems?

Very reliable for external elements: terminal blocks, bushings, tap changers and radiators. It can detect hot spots before they cause visible failure. However, it does not detect internal faults in the core or windings that do not yet generate surface heat. Thermography is complementary to DGA and electrical testing, it does not replace them.

What is the degree of polymerization (DP) of paper?

The DP is the average number of glucose units in the cellulose chains of the insulating paper. On new paper: DP 1,000–1,200. With aging, DP decreases irreversibly. At DP < 200 the paper has lost ~50% of mechanical strength. 2-Furfuraldehyde in oil (2-FAL > 1 ppm) is the non-invasive indicator most correlated with low PD.

What level of dielectric rigidity is unacceptable?

According to IEC 60422: for MV (up to 36 kV) the minimum is 30 kV/mm to continue operating. For HV (72.5 kV+) the minimum is 50 kV/mm. A value less than 20 kV/mm at any voltage level is an emergency that requires removal from service and immediate action on the oil.

What includes a complete predictive maintenance program?

Three levels: (1) Continuous monitoring: temperature, oil level, conservative pressure and Buchholz alarm. (2) Periodic inspection (semi-annual/annual): IR, Megger + PI, physical analysis of the oil (rigidity, humidity, acidity) and DGA. (3) Deep diagnosis (every 3–5 years): delta tangent, FRA to detect deformation of devanados and paper DP when 2–FAL > 1 ppm.
