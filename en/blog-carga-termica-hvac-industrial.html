How to Calculate Thermal Load for Industrial HVAC: ASHRAE Methodology and Common Mistakes in EPC Projects

The number that defines the size of the entire system — and where a 20% error in the calculation turns into oversized equipment, excessive energy consumption or worse: a process without sufficient cooling.

In an industrial HVAC EPC project, the thermal load calculation report is the first document that is prepared and the one that conditions all subsequent decisions: what equipment is purchased, how much energy the plant consumes, how robust the installation is. And yet it is also the document where errors are made most frequently — errors that are only discovered when the industrial process no longer reaches temperature, or when the chiller operates at 40% of its nominal capacity because the building never needed what was calculated.

1. The ton of refrigeration: the unit that defines everything

Before calculating any thermal load, it is necessary to be clear about the reference unit of the industrial refrigeration system.

Fundamental equivalence 1 TR (ton of refrigeration) = 12,000 BTU/h = 3.517 kW Historical origin: heat required to melt 1 short ton (907 kg) of ice in 24 hours at 0 °C.

This equivalence is the bridge between the thermal calculation (in BTU/h) and the selection of electrical equipment. A 100 TR chiller consumes on average between 90 and 120 kW of electricity depending on its COP (Performance Coefficient) — a critical data for the electrical engineering of the EPC project.

Industrial buildings

50 – 500 TR

Typical range for industrial buildings, production rooms and temperature-controlled warehouses.

Clean rooms / Pharmacist

20 – 150 TR

High internal load due to process equipment. Strict temperature and relative humidity requirements (±1°C, ±5% RH).

Industrial data centers

100 – 2 000 TR

Almost exclusively electrical load (servers, UPS). PUE as a design energy efficiency metric.

2. ASHRAE CLTD/CLF Method: The Six Components of Thermal Load

The CLTD (Cooling Load Temperature Difference) / CLF (Cooling Load Factor) method of the ASHRAE Fundamentals Manual is the industry standard for calculating cooling load in industrial buildings. It decomposes the total load into six heat sources that are calculated separately and added to obtain the design load.

Component 1 — Envelope Transmission

Heat that enters the space through walls, ceilings and floors due to temperature difference between exterior and interior.

Transmission gain Q_env = U × A × CLTD U = thermal transmission of the wall or ceiling (BTU/(h·ft2 •F)] , A = area [ft2] , CLTD = temperature difference of cooling load adjusted by latitude, month, hour, surface color and interior-outdoor difference.

The CLTD is a corrected version of the temperature difference that incorporates the effect of thermal storage of the wall mass — a 20 cm concrete wall does not transmit midday heat instantaneously: it does so with a delay of several hours. For an industrial warehouse with a metal roof in Monterrey, the profit per roof may be the dominant component.

Component 2 — Solar radiation through windows and skylights

Solar gain through windows Q_solar = SC × A_vidrio × SHGF × CLF SC = Shading Coefficient of glass (1.0 = clear glass, 0.4–0.6 = reflective glass) | SHGF = maximum solar gain factor by orientation and latitude [BTU/(h·ft²)] | CLF = fraction of time of day.

In industrial buildings with skylights or windows facing west, this component can represent 15–25% of the total load during peak hours (14:00–17:00 p.m.).

Component 3 — Heat of illumination

Lighting gain Q_ilum = W_ilum × FUT × FES × CLF W_ilum = installed power of luminaires [W] , FUT = use factor (fracking of luminaries on) , FES = specification factor (0.82 for luminaire embedded with diffuser, 1.0 for luminaries open) , CLF = storage factor by type of construction.

Component 4 — Occupant heat

Each person in an industrial space at a comfort temperature (22–26 °C) generates:

Heat sensitive

- 70 W / person

Convection and conduction into the air. Varies with activity level: 50 W at rest, 120 W at moderate activity (production line work).

Latent heat

- 45 W / person

Evaporation of sweat and respiration. It increases significantly with the temperature of the space and the level of physical activity.

Component 5 — Heat from process equipment (the most critical in industry)

Gain from process equipment Q_equipo = P_nominal × FUM × FLM P_nominal = nominal power of the equipment [W] | FUM = usage factor over time (fraction of operating hours) | FLM = load factor on the motor (fraction of the nominal power consumed in real operation). For electric motors: typical FUM 0.75–0.85, FLM 0.65–0.80.
The most costly mistake: In industrial plants, process equipment is often the most thermally charged component — exceeding in many cases the sum of envelope + lighting + people. Skipping them or underestimating them produces a fully sub-sized HVAC system. VMS Energy conducts a survey of all machinery installed (or planned) with its rated power and operating cycles before start the calculation.

Component 6 — Ventilation and infiltrations

Outdoor ventilation air (required by ASHRAE 62.1 — Ventilation for Acceptable Indoor Air Quality) enters at a different temperature and humidity than the interior space. This component includes both the sensible heat and the latent heat of the outside air:

Ventilation load (simplified) Q_sens_vent = 1.1 × CFM × ΔT | Q_lat_vent = 0.68 × CFM × ΔW CFM = outdoor air flow [ft³/min] | ΔT = temperature difference between outside and inside [°F] | ΔW = specific humidity difference [gr/lb of dry air]. The constants 1.1 and 0.68 are for standard air density conditions at sea level.

The total design load is the sum of the six components, evaluated at the time and conditions in which the simultaneous maximum occurs:

Total cooling load Q_total = Q_env + Q_solar + Q_illumination + Q_occupants + Q_equipment + Q_ventilation Converted to TR by dividing by 12,000 [BTU/h]. VMS Energy adds a safety factor of 5–10% on the total sum depending on the criticality of the process.

3. ASHRAE 0.4% design conditions for the main industrial cities of Mexico

ASHRAE 0.4% design conditions represent the dry bulb temperature (Tbs) that is Exceeds only 0.4% of the hours of the year — equivalent to approximately 35 hours per year. It is the standard criterion for the calculation of maximum summer load in industrial facilities where the interruption of the process has high cost.

To calculate the latent load, the design Tbh is required. In Ciudad del Carmen (coastal area), the Tbh of 27 °C generates a very high latent load that dominates the selection of the type of equipment.

4. Basic psychrometry: sensible load and latent load

Psychrometry is the study of the properties of humid air. It is the conceptual basis for understanding what part of the thermal load raises the temperature of the air (sensible heat) and what part changes its moisture content (latent heat).

Heat sensitive

Change temperature, not humidity

Conduction through walls, heat from electrical equipment, solar radiation. It is measured in °C. The HVAC equipment handles this by cooling the air below its design indoor temperature.

Latent heat

Change humidity, not temperature

Evaporation of sweat, processes with steam or water, infiltration of humid outside air. It is measured in gr/kg of dry air. The equipment removes it by condensing the moisture on the cold coil.

The relationship between the two defines the Sensible Heat Ratio (SHR):

Sensitive Heat Ratio SHR = Q_sensible / (Q_sensible + Q_latente) Typical SHR: 0.75–0.85 in dry industrial buildings. 0.60–0.70 in coastal areas or humid processes. <0.60 in clean rooms with steam injection processes.

The SHR defines the slope of the air process line on the psychometric chart and therefore what supply temperature and what temperature of the cooling temp (ADP — Appar 1989 Dew Point) the equipment requires. A machine selected only by capacity (TR) without considering the HRS may not be able to control relative humidity within the range of the process.

5. Effect of altitude on HVAC equipment capacity

This factor is critical in Mexico, where key industrial cities such as Guadalajara (1,552 m), Querétaro (1,826 m) and Mexico City (2,240 m) are located at significant altitudes. At higher altitudes, atmospheric pressure decreases, which reduces the air density and mass flow of distribution systems — and therefore the cooling capacity.

Altitude correction factor (approximate) F_alt = 1 - (altitud_m / 300) × 0.01 Rule of thumb: ~1% capacity loss for every 300 m altitude above sea level. For exact values, use manufacturer's tables or ASHRAE software.

Practical example for a project in Guadalajara (1,552 m):

Parameter At sea level Guadalajara (1,552 m) CDMX (2,240 m)
Altitude factor 1.000 0.948 0.925
Real capacity of a chiller of 100 nominal TR 100.0 TR 94.8 TR 92.5 TR
Real capacity of a nominal 35 TR chiller 35.0 TR 33.2 TR 32.4 TR
Frequent error: quote a chiller with the nominal capacity of the catalog without asking the manufacturer for the performance curve at the site altitude. VMS Energy always requests the performance sheet corrected at altitude, hot water input temperature and cold water temperature design of the specific project.

6. The 5 most frequent errors in the calculation of industrial thermal load

In reviewing industrial HVAC projects, VMS Energy repeatedly identifies the following engineering errors:

Mistake #1

Incorrect weather conditions or generic data

Using climate conditions from a different city, weather data from outdated databases, or applying sea level conditions to projects at altitude. It is also common to use the annual average temperature instead of the ASHRAE 0.4% design conditions.

Correction: Always use the ASHRAE Handbook — Fundamentals, Climate Design pit table for the exact site of the project. For ASHRAE data-free sites, interpolar sites or use data from the nearest MMS weather station.
Mistake #2

Underestimate or ignore the load of process equipment

Calculate the load with the process specifications under normal conditions and forget about production peaks, the simultaneous start-up of several pieces of equipment or the loading of auxiliary equipment (air compressors, UPS, transformers). On a pharmaceutical line, process equipment can be 50–65% of the total load.

Correction: Survey in the field (or with the machinery supplier) the real installed power of each piece of equipment, its FLM and FUM under full production conditions. Include all transformers and UPS within the climate-controlled space.
Mistake #3

Ignore latent load in coastal areas or humid processes

Select equipment only based on total TRs without checking SHR. In regions with design Tbh higher than 24 °C (Ciudad del Carmen, Veracruz, Tampico, Manzanillo), the latent load can represent 30–40% of the total load. Equipment without sufficient latent capacity cannot maintain the relative humidity of the process within specification even if it controls the temperature.

Correction: Calculate the SHR of the space and verify that the selected equipment can operate at that SHR at the site conditions. Consider dedicated dehumidification (desiccant wheel) in processes with SHR < 0.65.
Mistake #4

Do not apply capacity derating by altitude

Specify equipment at catalog rated capacity (at sea level) without requesting performance data from the manufacturer at the actual project altitude. In Querétaro (1,826 m), a team of 100 nominal TR delivers approximately 93.9 real TR — a deficit of 6.1% that can be the difference between controlling the process or not.

Correction: Always request the manufacturer's data sheet with corrected capacity at: site altitude, water input temperature (condensation or evaporation), design ice water temperature, and design mass flow.
Mistake #5

Do not provide for N+1 redundancy in critical applications

Install a single chiller or air handling unit for the entire process load. If the equipment fails — an eventuality with a non-negligible probability in equipment that operates 24/7 — all production stops. In the pharmaceutical, food and semiconductor industries, a line shutdown due to HVAC can cost tens of times the cost of redundant equipment.

Correction: Design with N+1 configuration: two units of 60–65% of the total load each. The backup unit can operate at partial load sustaining the process until repair or replacement of the failed equipment. Evaluate the cost of redundancy vs. the cost of a production stoppage.

7. Case: pharmaceutical plant in Jalisco — from 1,200 m² to 2×35 TR N+1

Documented EPC case

Pharmaceutical production laboratory — Guadalajara, Jalisco (1,552 m)

Description: Production room for oral solids (tablets and capsules), conditioned area of 1,200 m². ISO 8 classification (filling area) / ISO 7 (weighing room). Design temperature 22 ±1 °C, RH 45 ±5%.

Process survey data:

  • Tabletizers (3 sets): 15 kW nominal c/u, FLM 0.75, FUM 0.85
  • Granulators (2 units): 22 nominal kW each, FLM 0.70, FUM 0.80
  • Freeze dryers (1 unit): 45 kW nominal, FLM 0.90, FUM 0.70
  • LED lighting: 28 W/m² × 1 200 m² = 33.6 kW total, FES 0.82
  • Occupants: 35 people on peak shift, moderate activity (100 W sensitive + 55 W latent)
  • ASYRAE 62.1 ventilation: 8 renovations/hour = 8 × 1 200 m2 × 3.5 m = 33 600 m3/h

Summary of load calculation (Guadalajara ASHRAE 0.4% design conditions):

Process equipment
47%
Ventilation
21%
Lighting
14%
Occupants
10%
Envelope
6%
Solar
2%

Calculation result:

  • Total sensitive load: 176,400 BTU/h
  • Total latent load: 62,100 BTU/h (SHR = 0.74)
  • Total gross load: 238,500 BTU/h = 19.9 TR
  • Applied safety factor (10%): +2.0 TR
  • Design load: 21.9 TR (at sea level)

Correction for Guadalajara altitude (1,552 m, factor 0.948):

  • Corrected design load: 21.9 TR / 0.948 = 23.1 TR required equipment capacity at sea level
  • However, this is just the production room. The entire building HVAC system (store areas, corridors, QA offices) adds an additional 38.9 TR
  • Total system load: 62.0 TR

Selected solution — N+1 configuration:

  • 2 × Screw Chiller of 35 TR nominal (actual capacity in Guadalajara: 33.2 TR each)
  • Normal operation: 1 chiller at 94% load (62 TR total / 2 = 31 TR each of 33.2 TR available)
  • Chiller failure: 1 chiller at 100% = 33.2 TR → maintains ≈54% of design capacity — enough to sustain ISO 7/8 critical room temperature during maintenance response time
  • Refrigerant: R-454B (GWP 466) — early compliance with the Kigali Amendment
  • Manufacturer nominal COP: 4.2 at design conditions → electrical consumption: 2 × (35 TR × 3.517 kW/TR) / 4.2 = 58.6 kW in normal operation
  • BMS integration: BACnet IP, points: Tchw_supply, Tchw_return, Tcond, kW, compressor alarms
Lesson from the case: The 1,200 m² pharmaceutical room is not a scaled commercial HVAC project. 47% of the load comes from the process equipment — data that only emerges from a survey in the field with the production equipment. Without that survey, the load could have been calculated at 9–12 TR using the area (W/m²) method alone, and the resulting system would have been radically insufficient.

8. Frequently asked questions

What is a ton of cooling and how many kW is it?

One tonne of cooling (TR) equals 12 000 BTU/h = 3.517 kW. It is the standard reference unit for the sizing of chillers, precision equipment and process systems. The conversion to kW is key to integrating HVAC load balancing with the electrical engineering of the EPC project.

What thermal load calculation method does VMS Energy use?

VMS Energy applies the method CLTD/CLF This method calculates separately: enveloping transmission (U×A×CLTD), solar radiation (SC×A×SHGF×CLF), lighting, occupants ( > 70 W sensitive + 45 W latent/person), process equipment (P×FUM×FLM) and ventilation/infiltrations. The sum of the six components gives the total design load in BTU/h or TR.

What are the design conditions ASHRAE 0.4% for industrial cities in Mexico?

The most relevant design conditions are: Monterrey 40 °C / 22 °C Tbh, Ciudad del Carmen 35 °C / 27 °C Tbh, Guadalajara 33 °C / 19 °C Tbh, Querétaro 32 °C / 18 °C Tbh, CDMX 27 °C (at 2 240 m altitude). Hermosillo registers the most extreme condition in Mexico: 43 °C Tbs. These conditions are the basis of the summer maximum load calculation.

How does altitude affect the capacity of HVAC equipment?

The standard rule is approximately 1% capacity loss per 300 m On sea level. In Guadalajara (1,552 m): factor 0.948. In CDMX (2,240 m): factor 0.925. VMS Energy always requests the manufacturer's corrected data sheet by altitude before specifying the equipment — the standard catalogue reports capabilities at sea level.

What is the sensitive load and latent load on HVAC?

The sensitive load is heat that changes the air temperature without changing its moisture content (wall drive, electrical equipment, radiation). latent load is the heat associated with the change of humidity of the air (sweat evaporation, steam processes, wet air infiltration). Sensible Heat Ratio (SHR) is the fraction of sensitive load on the total load — defines the type of equipment required and its dehumidification capacity.

What are the most common errors in industrial thermal load calculation?

The five most common mistakes: (1) using incorrect weather conditions or no altitude adjustment; (2) omit or underestimate the loading of process equipment; (3) ignore latent load in coastal zones or humid processes; (4) do not apply altitude capacity derating when selecting equipment; and (5) do not provide for N+1 redundancy in critical process applications. Each of these errors can result in an undersized system or unnecessary energy waste for life.

Why are two N+1 drivers specified instead of one with greater capacity?

Settings N+1 offers three advantages in industrial plants: (1) operational redundancy — if a steel mill fails, the second maintains between 50 and 80% of the capacity, avoiding total production failure; (2) partial load efficiency — two units at 60–70% of their optimal POPs are more efficient than one at 100%; (3) maintenance flexibility — preventive maintenance can be provided without stopping the process. It is standard practice in pharmaceutical, food, frigerians and industrial data centers.
