How to Design an Industrial Motor Control Center: IEC 61439, Starters and Communication Protocols

August 25, 2026 16 min read VMS Energy Engineering

A Engine Control Center (MCC) is not another board. It is the nervous system of the industrial process: it concentrates the start, protection, monitoring and control of each engine of the plant in a single assembly designed, manufactured and tested under the IEC 61439 standard. An incorrect specification — the wrong construction type, the insufficient form of separation, a starter Oversized or a protocol incompatible with the PLC—may result in unplanned stoppages, risk of electric arc for staff or equipment that never reach their energy efficiency potential.

This guide covers the complete engineering process: from the definition of the construction type and the IEC 61439-2 separation form, to the selection of the starter, the coordination of Type 2 protections and the choice of the industrial communication protocol. At the end you will find the real case of the CTG Chankanaab project and the most frequently asked technical questions of the specification process.

1. Construction type: fixed, plug-in or drawout

The first design decision defines the maintenance access mechanism and the life cycle cost of the MCC. There are three fundamental architectures:

Type Withdrawal mechanism Bus under voltage during maintenance IEC 61439 Forms available Key advantage
Fixed Requires power cut No — total unemployment Form 1, 2a/2b, 3a/3b Lower initial cost
Plug-in Without tools, contacts are disconnected when extracting Yes — energized loop Form 2b, 3a/3b, 4a Quick change of spare module
Drawout (removable) Carriage in 3 positions: connected / test / disconnected Yes — rest of the active JCC Form 4b exclusively Warm maintenance, test position
VMS engineering rule In projects with more than 12 process motors and where production stoppage exceeds $50,000/hour, the drawout type pays for itself in less than 18 months compared to the plug-in type. In Oil & Gas and power generation, drawout Form 4b is the specification standard.

2. Forms of separation IEC 61439-2: Form 1 to Form 4b

Separation forms define the level of physical isolation between the internal parts of the MCC: bus bars, functional units (UF) and external cable terminals. The higher the number, the greater the safety during maintenance and the higher the manufacturing cost.

Form 1

No internal separation

All in a common compartment: bars, UF and terminals accessible when opening the door. Not in applications with frequent maintenance under voltage.

Non-critical auxiliaries with low maintenance frequency.
Form 2a / 2b

Separate UF bars

Physical barrier between bars and UF. Form 2a: Adjacent FUs share compartment. Form 2b: each UF in its own compartment.

Food & Beverage, HVAC, treatment plants.
Form 3a / 3b

Separate bar terminals

Adds separation between external terminals and bars. In 3b there is also separation between adjacent FUs. Standard in medium manufacturing.

Automotive, mining, industrial distribution substations.
Form 4a / 4b

Total separation

Bars, UF and terminals in 100% independent compartments. Form 4b: separate UF terminals — allows hot drawout.

Oil & Gas, generation, petrochemical — non-stop maintenance.

The critical difference between Form 3b and Form 4b

In Form 3b the cable terminal compartment is separate from the bars, but the terminals share space with the functional unit. When the technician opens that cubicle to check a cable connection, he or she is exposed to the live parts of the active UF. In Form 4b the terminals have their own compartment independent of the UF: the cable terminal can be accessed without risk of contact with the contactor, relay or VFD that continues to operate. This difference is why Oil & Gas specifies Form 4b as a minimum, not Form 3b.

Practical decision criterion

The simplified decision tree: if the process requires variable speed → VFD mandatory. If the process is fixed speed and the power is greater than 75 kW with boot without load → Y-Δ or soft-Sterger. If the load has mechanical impact on starter (pumps with water column, loaded belts) → soft-starter. If the power is less than 22 kW and the network tolerates it → DOL. In the face of the doubt between soft-starter and VFD, VFD always produces more value in the long term; the soft-starters is mainly justified by cost and less generation of amonics in regime.

4. Integrated protections and Type 2 coordination

Each functional unit of the MCC combines a chain of protection devices. The IEC 60947-4-1 standard establishes two levels of coordination between the short-circuit device and the contactor/overload relay:

DeviceFunctionTypical configurationStandard
MCCB Long-life short circuit and overcurrent protection Ir = 1.0–1.25 × Motor In; Im = 10–15 × In IEC 60947-2
Contactor Opening and closing maneuver; does not interrupt short circuit Category AC-3 (squirrel cage motors) IEC 60947-4-1
Thermal relay Continuous overload protection (class 10/20/30) Setting = 1.0–1.15 × In; class 10 for short start IEC 60947-4-1
Motor protector (PKZ/MS) Combine MCCB + thermal relay in a single device Direct current adjustment; Type 2 coordination guaranteed by factory IEC 60947-4-1
Programmable relay (ANSI 49/51/46) Motor differential protection, phase imbalance, locked rotor For motors > 75 kW or critical process motors IEEE C37.96
Type 2 vs Type 1 coordination — what really matters in production Type 1: After short circuit, the contactor can be destroyed. The MCC can be left out of service until the spare part is obtained (hours or days). Type 2: The MCCB interrupts before the I2t energy destroys the contactor. Contacts may have mild welding, but they are manually separable — the starter returns to operation in minutes. VMS Energy verifies Type 2 in detail engineering using the coordination tables published by the manufacturer for each MCCB-352or-reloader combination.

5. Industrial communication protocols

The integration of the MCC with the plant control system (PLC/SCADA) determines real-time visibility of the status of each motor: current, voltage, protection status, start count and winding temperature. The choice of protocol depends on the existing PLC platform, the number of nodes and determinism requirements.

Modbus RTU/TCP

The universal standard

Modbus RTU over RS-485 is compatible with all PLCs on the market. Simple, robust, license-free. Modbus TCP moves the same protocol over standard Ethernet, eliminating the RS-485/Ethernet gateway. Ideal for legacy systems or when budget limits the use of more advanced protocols.

6. NFPA 70E Arc Flash Analysis

Arc flash analysis determines the incident energy (cal/cm²) to which the technician is exposed when working in a live MCC cubicle. It is a safety requirement — not an option — for any facility where personnel perform electrical maintenance on energized equipment.

IEEE Methodology 1584-2018

The IEEE 1584-2018 model is the de facto standard for arc calculation in LV (<15 kV). The input parameters are: system voltage, short-circuit current on the MCC bus (kA symmetrical), electrode type (VCB, VCBB, HCB, HOA), cabinet dimensions, working distance, and arc clearance time (time it takes for upstream protection to interrupt the fault).

The parameter that the electrical engineer can control to reduce the incident energy is the time of clearing of the arc– By adding a Bus Zone Protection relay or reducing the trip times of upstream MCCBs, the protection interrupts the arc in milliseconds — reducing incident energy by orders of magnitude. In existing installations, adding a bus zone relay is the most economical way to downgrade PPE without replacing the MCC.

7. Regulatory framework of the industrial MCC

IEC 61439-1 LV switchgear assemblies — General requirements: design and routine tests, temperature limits, verification of separation form.
IEC 61439-2 Power Sets — MCC-specific extension. Defines cubicles, Forms 1 to 4b and maintenance access requirements.
IEC 60947-4-1 AC motor starters. Employment categories, Type 1 and Type 2 coordination, minimum short circuit current, operational life cycle of the contactor.
NOM-001-SEDE-2012 Article 430 (motors): overcurrent protection, sizing of supply conductors and protection of branch circuits.
NFPA 70E Electrical safety. Arc flash study, PPE categories and mandatory warning labels in each MCC cubicle.
UL 508A Industrial control bricks. Required for export to the USA or when the customer specifies UL certification for the complete assembly.

8. Common errors in the specification of a MCC

Error 1 — Specifying Form 3b when the process requires Form 4b

The engineer chooses Form 3b to reduce cost. During maintenance, the technician opens the terminal cubicle and is exposed to the active UF. First intervention under voltage results in electric arc incident.

Correction: Define the form of separation based on the process continuity requirement — if the MCC must remain live during terminal maintenance, the minimum specification is Form 4b. The cost overrun compared to Form 3b rarely exceeds 20% of the board.
Error 2 — Select Y-Δ for a water column pump

The MCC starts the star pump motor. When switching to triangle the pump already has pressure in the line. The switching transient generates a current peak that exceeds the DOL and the star contactor is destroyed at the first load start.

Correction: For centrifugal pumps with back pressure or any load with resistive starting torque, specify soft-starter with adjusted acceleration ramp. The Y-Δ is only suitable when mechanical no-load starting is guaranteed.
Error 3 — VFD installed without LCL filter in network with high sensitivity to harmonics

8 75 kW VFDs are installed in the MCC without input filters. The THDi amounts to 42% on the main bus. The protection relays of other equipment begin to trip due to false positives. The substation registers transformer overheating.

Correction: Carry out the harmonics study before specifying the VFDs. If the power sum of VFDs exceeds 20% of the transformer capacity, specify LCL filters or line reactors to limit THDi < 5% in accordance with IEEE 519.
Error 4 — Forgetting altitude derating in installations above 1,000 m.a.s.l.

The MCC is installed in Durango at 1,890 meters above sea level. The bus ampacity and thermal dissipation capacity of VFDs and soft-starters are not corrected. After 6 months, the VFDs begin to trip due to overtemperature.

Correction: IEC 61439 requires derating for altitudes above 1 000 m: the nominal current of the assembly is reduced by approximately 0.5% for each additional 100 m. Specify VFDs and soft-starters with service factor corrected for the site altitude.
Error 5 — Communication protocol incompatible with the existing PLC

The MCC is manufactured with devices that only support Profibus DP. The customer has an Allen-Bradley ControlLogix PLC. The integrator installs a Profibus/EtherNet/IP gateway that adds 400 ms of latency. The SCADA cannot monitor emergency starts in real time.

Correction: Define the communication protocol in the basic engineering phase, before specifying the MCC devices. Verify native compatibility of each selected starter/VFD with the required protocol — do not assume additional firmware compatibility.

9. Real case: MCC drawout Form 4b for CTG Chankanaab

Oil & Gas / Power Generation — Playa del Carmen, Quintana Roo

Control of auxiliary motors of industrial turbogenerators

The CTG Chankanaab project required the control and protection of the auxiliary motors of a turbogenerator system: lubrication pumps, oil cooling pumps, room fans and control valve actuator motors. The most critical constraint of the project: the backup generators could not be stopped during commissioning or maintenance of the MCC.

VMS Energy designed a 4b BAC type Pisout Form with 24 removable carts. ABB PSRs from the lubrication pumps were set up with an 8-second acceleration ramp to remove the battering strokes in the oil system. Communication was implemented in Profibus DP with the TCP TCP with the plant control SCADA, allowing for current, temperature and status of each engine from the control room without needing personnel in the electric room.

The position of Test The PV was decisive: it allowed to verify the automatic start sequences, the safety interlocks and the signs of the instrumentation with the active PLC — without energize the engines — reducing the commissioning time by 40% compared to the original plan.

Frequently asked technical questions

Why is Form 4b and no Form 3b specified for a MCC in Oil & Gas plant?

The key difference is what is exposed when the cable terminals are accessed. In Form 3b, the terminals share a compartment with the functional unit: when opening the cubicle to check a connection, the technician is exposed to the live parts of the active contactor or VFD. In Form 4b, the terminals have their own compartment completely independent of the UF — a cable can be checked or reconnected without risk of contact with the still operating UF. In Oil & Gas, where production stoppage has a very high cost and the MCC must remain under voltage during terminal maintenance, Form 4b is the minimum acceptable. The extra cost compared to Form 3b is 15–25%, largely offset by the elimination of plant stoppages.

When is a soft-starter better than a VFD, if the VFD controls the start current better?

The soft-starter is justified over the VFD in three scenarios: (1) Cost: a soft-starter for the same power costs 40–60% less than an equivalent VFD. (2) Harmonics in regime: The VFD generates THDi of 30–80% continuously; the soft-starter only generates amonics during the seconds of start—if the network already has high harmonic distortion, adding VFDs without filters worsens the energy quality of the entire plant. (3) Maintenance simplicity: The VFD has electronic cards sensitive to temperature, vibration and humidity; the soft-starter is more robust in aggressive industrial environments. The soft-starters is the optimal choice when the process does not require variable speed in regime: centrifugal pumps at fixed flow rate, reciprocating compressors, constant speed mills.

What is Type 2 coordination and why does it matter in production?

Type 2 coordination according to IEC 60947-4-1 requires that after a short circuit, the contactor and overload relay do not suffer damage that prevents their reuse — there can only be light welding of the contactor contacts, provided they are manually separable. In practice: the MCCB or associated fuse must interrupt the short circuit current before the energy I²t destroys the contactor. The alternative, Type 1 coordination, only requires that there is no risk to people — the contactor can be destroyed. In industrial production, a destroyed contactor implies a stoppage until a replacement is obtained (hours or days in remote regions). With Type 2, the technician separates the contacts and the starter is back up and running in minutes. VMS Energy verifies Type 2 in detailed engineering with the manufacturer's coordination tables.

How is the electric arc EPP category determined for a MCC?

Arc flash analysis per NFPA 70E and IEEE Model 1584-2018 calculates the incident energy in cal/cm² over the working distance for each cubicle. The process: (1) Network model with bus short circuit data. (2) Arc current calculation. (3) Clearing time: How long it takes for the upstream protection to clear the fault. (4) Incident energy = f(I²arc × t_clearance, distance). The result classifies each cubicle into PPE Category 1 (≤4 cal/cm²) to Category 4 (≤40 cal/cm²). Cubicles with more than 40 cal/cm² require protection redesign — typically adding a bus zone relay that reduces clearance time from seconds to milliseconds, lowering the incident energy to Category 1 or 2.

Can you add a VFD to an existing fixed-type MCC without replacing the board?

It depends on two factors: available space and thermal capacity of the cabinet. A VFD generates significant heat (losses of 2–3% of rated power); an enclosure that was not designed for that dissipation may exceed the thermal limits of IEC 61439. Additionally, the original protective MCCB may not be suitable for Type 2 coordination with the VFD — some VFDs require semiconductor fuses instead of MCCBs. The correct procedure: verify the thermal capacity of the cubicle with the new VFD heat sink, confirm Type 2 coordination with the manufacturer, and update the arc flash study, since the VFD changes the available short circuit current. If the conditions are not met, it is safer to replace the affected cubicle rather than the entire switchboard.

What is the practical difference between Profibus DP and PROFINET for a new MCC project?

Profibus DP is Siemens' mature protocol (IEC 61158): deterministic, proven for decades, with guaranteed spare parts. Requires specific shielded twisted pair cable (150 Ω) and active terminators at each end of the segment. PROFINET is the successor to standard Cat6 Ethernet with star topology (PROFINET managed switch). PROFINET eliminates the length and node restrictions of Profibus, allows integrated remote diagnostics from the TIA Portal, and with IRT achieves deterministic cycles of 1 ms. For a new MCC in a Siemens project with S7-1500/1200 (TIA Portal): PROFINET if the plant has existing Ethernet infrastructure. If there is legacy S7-300/400 PLC: Profibus DP is easier to integrate without updating firmware or adding PROFINET switches.

What technical deliverables should include the complete MCC industrial's wire?

The complete technical dossier under IEC 61439 must include: (1) As-built electrical plans: updated single-line, point-to-point control diagram, cubicle layout with ID of each UF. (2) Calculation memory: short circuit current on each bus, protection selection with Type 2 coordination, bus ampacity verification. (3) FAT report: dielectric test (2U₀+1,000 V for 1 min), insulation resistance measurement (>1 MΩ), sequence verification with simulator. (4) SAT report on site. (5) NFPA 70E arc flash study with labels installed. (6) Archived configuration of starters and VFDs (manufacturer's project file). (7) Preventive maintenance plan: connection torque, relay calibration, semi-annual thermography. (8) List of recommended critical spare parts with part number.
