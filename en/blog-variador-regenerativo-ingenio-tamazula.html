The challenge: moving high inertia loads without punishing the network

A sugar mill is a heavy rotary equipment plant. Mills, centrifuges and pumps are loads of high inertia: It is very difficult to start them and, once in motion, it is difficult to stop them. When that equipment starts directly, the blow is distributed throughout the system.

Direct starting generates current and torque peaks that punish the motor, the mechanical couplings and the plant's electrical network. And when the equipment decelerates, all the accumulated kinetic energy is lost: it is dissipated as heat in resistors or in the process itself. In an operation that runs seasonally and cannot afford stoppages, this repeated electrical stress translates into wear and tear, unplanned failures and energy that is paid for but not used.

The Tamazula Mill needed the opposite: soft starters, fine speed control and a way not to throw away the energy of the braking.

What is a regenerative variable And why does he change the rules?

A frequency variator (VFD) regulates the speed of an engine by adjusting the frequency and voltage it receives. That already solves the smooth start and variable speed. But a regenerative variable adds a decisive capacity: when the engine slows down and behaves like a generator, the variable takes that energy and the returns to the power grid instead of burning it in braking resistances.

In a high inertia load that brakes again and again, that difference matters. With a conventional variator, braking energy is converted into heat that must also be ventilated. With a regenerative one, that same energy returns to the system and is available for other plant consumption. Less heat, less waste and an energy bill that works in favor of ingenuity.

The question is not just how to move the equipment, but what to do with the power whenever it stops.

For a mill with certification and energy efficiency goals, regenerative braking stops being a technical extra and becomes an operational and financial argument.

The project: 300 HP Schneider Electric at the Tamazula Mill

VMS Energy implemented the complete project at the Ingenio Tamazula, Jalisco (2024): the selection and engineering of the regenerative variator of 300 HP Schneider Electric, its supply, installation on site and start-up with the process running. A single interlocutor from start to finish, without fragmenting responsibility among several suppliers.

The system began to operate with controlled acceleration ramps, speed adjusted to the real demand of the process and recovery of braking energy to the mill network. All with the drive's native protections active from startup.

What this means for your plant

If you operate an ingenuity, a food and beverage plant or any process with high inertia rotating equipment, the case of Tamazula leaves three practical readings:

  • Less electrical and mechanical stress It extends the life of the engine and reduces the risk of unplanned stoppages.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a regenerative variator and a normal frequency variator?

Both regulate the motor speed with a soft start, but the regenerative drive returns the energy generated during braking to the grid, instead of dissipating it as heat in resistors. For high-inertia loads that brake frequently, that represents real energy savings and less heat to manage.

What kind of equipment does a regenerative variator fit in an ingenuity?

It is especially useful in high inertia loads such as mills, centrifuges and pumps, where direct starting punishes the motor and the network, and where braking is frequent. In those cases soft start, speed control and energy recovery have the greatest impact.

Does VMS Energy only supply the variator or execute the entire project?

VMS Energy executes the comprehensive project under one responsibility: engineering, supply, installation and commissioning. In the case of Tamazula this included selecting the 300 HP Schneider Electric variator, its integration and commissioning with the operating process.
